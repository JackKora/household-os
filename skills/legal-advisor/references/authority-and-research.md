# Authority and Legal Research

Use this reference when a material conclusion depends on jurisdiction-specific, current, or historical law. Research only the point necessary to answer the user's question unless broader research was requested.

## Establish the scope

Identify the relevant:

- person or interest being advised
- country, state, locality, tribal jurisdiction, court, and agency
- locations of the people, property, transaction, conduct, and harm
- governing-law, venue, forum-selection, and arbitration terms
- date of the relevant conduct and date of the contemplated action
- procedural posture and known deadlines

The user's residence may not determine governing law. If jurisdiction cannot be resolved, give the durable general principle, explain the dependency, and avoid presenting a local conclusion as universal.

## Find and verify the relevant authority

Use an efficient path suited to the question. Secondary sources such as treatises, practice guides, law-review material, and reputable public explanations can be the fastest way to orient, learn the controlling terminology, identify disputed points, and discover primary authority. Then read and verify the primary sources that support each material conclusion.

Authority weight is jurisdiction- and proposition-specific, not a universal numbered hierarchy. Determine what controls the particular point in the relevant forum. That may require enacted constitutional, statutory, treaty, tribal, regulatory, or municipal text; binding opinions; procedural or evidentiary rules; local rules or standing orders; official forms and instructions; or administrative decisions. Distinguish controlling authority from persuasive cases, agency interpretations, enforcement guidance, and secondary analysis. Prefer official repositories for the final verification when available, but do not make official-source browsing the mandatory first step.

Pending legislation is not current law. A model rule, Restatement, uniform act, or model code is not binding unless adopted. For state versions of uniform laws, verify the enacted state text and amendments.

## Check time and validity

Determine which version of the law governed the event, not only the current version. Check enactment, amendment, effective date, repeal, savings, transition, retroactivity, emergency orders, and whether a rule was stayed or enjoined.

For cases:

- confirm the case exists in an official or reliable source
- read the opinion rather than relying on a search excerpt or summary
- distinguish the holding from dicta, a party's argument, a dissent, and a later characterization
- confirm the court, jurisdiction, precedential status, and procedural posture
- check subsequent history, reversal, vacatur, superseding authority, and negative treatment

If a reliable citator is unavailable, say that negative treatment was not comprehensively checked and avoid overstating certainty.

## Build the analysis

Separate:

- verified facts
- the user's assertions
- reasonable assumptions
- disputed or missing facts
- binding rules
- persuasive sources
- application or inference

Identify the relevant elements, burdens, defenses, exceptions, remedies, procedural prerequisites, and contrary argument. Use ranges or conditional conclusions when facts or law are unsettled. Do not calculate limitations, appeal, service, or response deadlines without verifying the trigger, counting convention, exclusions, tolling, extensions, and applicable local rules.

## Cite usefully

For a material legal proposition, cite the primary authority as close as practical to the conclusion. Include jurisdiction, relevant year or effective date, and a pinpoint section, rule, paragraph, or page when available. Link to the official source where possible.

Do not overwhelm a big-picture answer with a source dump. If the user asks for a quick explanation, provide the core source or offer the authority after the answer. Never invent a quotation, citation, docket event, form, deadline, or filing requirement.
